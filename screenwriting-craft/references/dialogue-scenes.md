# Dialogue & Scene Craft

Distilled from produced screenplays (read via SimplyScripts-linked archives) and NYFA's dialogue/scene teaching.

## Dialogue

- **Subtext is everything.** Characters talk *around* feelings, never about them — they imply, deflect, lie, joke, trail off. The classic amateur failure is on-the-nose dialogue ("As you know, we've been married ten years"). Test: if a line states a fact the audience already knows or a feeling they can already see, cut or rewrite it sideways.
- **Dialogue is action.** Every speech is a move: persuasion, attack, siege, seduction, retreat. If a line isn't doing something to someone, it doesn't belong.
- **Economy.** The most emotional lines are the shortest. "I wanna go home." / "I hope." Two words can end a two-hour story.
- **Distinct voices.** Voice = character. Cover the character cues and you should still tell who's talking (rhythm, vocabulary, sentence length, what they avoid saying).
- **Theme lines are aphorisms planted as dialogue, paid as plot.** A folksy maxim introduced casually in Act 1 becomes a story event by Act 3 — introduced as talk, resolved as action.
- **Pauses and body language carry as much meaning as words.** What a character *won't* say is the scene. Write the silence: the look away, the unfinished sentence, the subject change.
- **Repetition with shifting context.** One short line repeated 2–3 times, each time meaning something new (dismissal → cracking → surrender — the words never change; the delivery/context changes, so the meaning changes). The third landing is the tear moment.
- **Never lecture.** Dialogue that explains the story to the audience is "heavy exposition" — the single most amateur marker. Drip-feed; trust the audience to add 2+2.

## Scenes

- **Enter late, leave early.** Open mid-action (no arriving, no greetings — cut "Hello, how are you?" → start at "You're late."). Exit on the turn, not after it — no lingering.
- **One clear purpose per scene.** Name it: this scene exists to force X to confront Y. If you can't name the purpose, cut the scene.
- **Conflict in every scene.** Someone wants something and someone/something is in the way — even in quiet scenes (an argument fought *around* the person it's about).
- **Every scene turns.** A value shifts: hope→despair, trust→doubt, resolve→dread. No turn = no scene. End on the turn or the hook/question it creates.
- **Misdirection, planted in character logic.** A red herring works when every character (and the audience) reasonably reads it one way — then it means another. Never a cheat; always fair in hindsight.
- **Repetition with variation across scenes.** The same question asked three times with evolving answers tracks internal change visibly; a repeated prop/state change (a stamp going from REJECTED to APPROVED) makes the turn visual.
- **End on images, not speeches.** The final impression of a scene — and of the story — should be something seen, not something said.

## Pacing: Scene / Sequel (Swain)
- **Scene** = Goal → Conflict → Disaster (tension up).
- **Sequel** = Reaction → Dilemma → Decision (breathing room; motivates the next scene).
- Pacing lives in this alternation. Relentless incident exhausts; sequels are where warmth, grief, and decisions land. For older audiences especially: alternate high-tension beats with quiet reaction beats instead of stacking incident on incident.

## Scene checklist (NYFA-derived)
Who / what / when / where / **why** (what's the point of this scene?) — all answered. Description trimmed to fewest concrete, filmable words. Emotion rendered as objects and actions, never adjectives. Interior states shown through physical behavior.
