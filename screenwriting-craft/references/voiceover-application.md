# Applying Screenwriting Craft to Narrated YouTube Stories

The adaptation layer: everything in this skill translated for emotional narrated stories (8+ min, AI voiceover + generated visuals, senior 65+ audience). Works alongside the story engine's own rules — this skill supplies the *craft underneath* the beats.

## The 8-minute beat map (Save the Cat percentages → runtime)
| Beat | Timestamp | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Opening Image | 0:00 | One concrete object/scene in motion — no setup, no naming yet |
| Hook | 0:00–0:18 | Strange behavior + unanswered question (mid-crisis) |
| Theme Stated | ~0:25 | The lesson, spoken casually in narration |
| Catalyst | ~0:50 | The event that breaks the routine |
| Break into Two | ~1:35 | Committed — the story proper begins |
| Fun and Games | 1:35–4:00 | The promise of the premise; warmth builds |
| Midpoint (setback) | ~4:00 (55%) | False defeat — same goal, new cost; "new self" born |
| Bad Guys Close In | 4:00–6:00 | Pressure mounts |
| All Is Lost | ~6:00 (75%) | Lowest emotional point |
| Twist | ~5:35–6:00 (70%) | Reversal — the 2+2 reveal (see below) |
| Finale | ~6:30 | The choice; theme proven in action |
| 2-beat ending | ~7:30–8:00 | Setup line + ≤10-word quotable landing; then stop |

## The 5 voiceover laws
1. **Narrate the interior, never the exterior.** The voiceover's only legitimate jobs: (a) the character's emotional state, (b) hints of what's coming. Never describe what viewers already see — that duplicates the image track and insults the audience.
2. **Subtext rule.** Never narrate a feeling the viewer can infer. Not "He was sad" — describe observable behavior (head on paws, untouched food) and let the viewer feel it. Emotion lands harder for being unspoken.
3. **Understatement.** Plain sentences, stated facts, zero emotional adjectives. The flatter the telling, the harder the landing — the audience does the crying.
4. **Cold open on a visual.** Start with one concrete object in motion before naming anyone. Never burn the opening on setup; drip-feed backstory after the hook lands.
5. **One ending.** Build tempo to the last event, deliver the landing line, then stop. No epilogue, no moral restated twice.

## The twist: the 2+2 rule
At the ~70% reveal, never state the meaning. Give the audience the last two pieces and let them complete the picture themselves — "they'll love you for it." A twist that is *explained* feels cheap; a twist the viewer *assembles* feels profound. Withhold the obvious conclusion; the notes arrive as surprises.

## The refrain (repetition-to-break)
One short line (≤7 words) returning 2–3 times with shifting context: first as fact, second as ache, third as the tear moment. The words never change; the meaning changes. This is the voiceover equivalent of a musical motif — use sparingly (the anti-repetition system tracks refrains; a refrain used 3× is banned next batch).

## The plant-and-payoff object
One recurring object (book, feather, carving, collar) appears innocuous in minute one and returns transformed at the climax. Carry emotion in the object, not in adjectives. Bookend option: the Final Image echoes the Opening Image as proof of transformation.

## Character engine for animal stories
- **Want vs. need:** the dog's *want* (find the owner, get home, protect) vs. its *need* (learn to trust again, let go, accept love). The human's want/need mirrors or contrasts.
- **Ghost/wound:** what happened before minute one that shaped them. Surfaces through behavior, never exposition.
- **U-turn check:** by the end, the protagonist wants the opposite of what they wanted at the start (the stray who wanted to be left alone → wants to stay).
- Choices under pressure reveal character — engineer the climax as a *choice*, not an event happening to them.

## Pre-write gates (answer before drafting)
1. **Logline test:** "Someone wants something and something/someone gets in their way" — with emotional consequences of failure. If you can't fill that sentence, the idea isn't ready.
2. **Clean line of action:** the protagonist's visible objective + plan. No motivation → no conflict → no audience care.
3. **Scene-purpose check:** every beat advances plot or reveals character (ideally both) and turns a value.
4. **Setup audit:** every payoff in Act 3 is planted in Act 1. If the ending feels unearned, the beginning is under-planted — fix the setup, not the ending.

## Scene/Sequel pacing for a 65+ audience
Alternate high-tension beats (Goal → Conflict → Disaster) with sequel beats (Reaction → Dilemma → Decision) — quiet aftermath where warmth and tears land. Never stack incident on incident; the audience needs breathing room to feel.

## Practical exercises (NYFA-adapted)
1. Write the hook three ways: before / during / after the crisis — keep only the "during" version.
2. Write the twist twice: once explained, once as 2+2 — keep the unexplained one.
3. Write the ending, then cut the last two sentences. If it still lands, ship it.
4. Read the voiceover aloud at ~120 wpm; any sentence you stumble on gets rewritten.
5. Cover the visuals: does the narration still work as pure audio? If it only works with the pictures, it's duplicating them — rewrite to the interior.
