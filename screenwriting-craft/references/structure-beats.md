# Structure & Beats

Distilled from Save the Cat, Syd Field's 3-act model, NYFA's 8-sequence breakdown, and Vogler's hero's journey. Percentages are the portable part — they scale to any runtime.

## Three-act skeleton (Syd Field)
- **Act I — Setup (~25%):** world, character, want; ends with **Plot Point I** — the event that launches the main conflict (point of no return).
- **Act II — Confrontation (~50%):** obstacles escalate; ends with **Plot Point II** — slingshots into the climax.
- **Act III — Resolution (~25%):** climax (hero acts on what the journey taught them — earned, not rescued), then one ending.

## Save the Cat — 15 beats (percentages, not page numbers)
1. **Opening Image** — the world as it is (0%).
2. **Theme Stated** (~5%) — the lesson, spoken casually early (often by a side character).
3. **Set-Up** (1–10%) — stakes, want, flaw established.
4. **Catalyst** (~10%) — the event that breaks the routine.
5. **Debate** (10–20%) — resist, hesitate, refuse.
6. **Break into Two** (20%) — committed; the story proper begins.
7. **B Story** (~22%) — the relationship/heart thread (where the theme lives).
8. **Fun and Games** (20–50%) — the promise of the premise; the trailer moments.
9. **Midpoint** (50%) — false victory or false defeat; same goal, new way to get there; stakes go personal.
10. **Bad Guys Close In** (50–75%) — pressure mounts, allies fray.
11. **All Is Lost** (75%) — lowest emotional point; "whiff of death."
12. **Dark Night of the Soul** (75–85%) — the protagonist wants to quit.
13. **Break into Three** (80%) — the answer returns (often from the B story); the hero synthesizes want + need.
14. **Finale** (80–99%) — the hero acts on what was learned; theme proven in action.
15. **Final Image** — mirrors the Opening Image as proof of transformation.

## The 8-sequence mini-movie map (NYFA)
Each act splits into ~4 sequences; every sequence ends on a new up/down step:
- **A:** Inciting incident → crisis/call to action.
- **B:** Big Decision committing to the adventure → end of Act 1.
- **C:** Fish-out-of-water in the new world; promise-of-the-premise moments.
- **D:** New tack → surprising **midpoint twist**; stakes raised, "new self" born.
- **E:** Changed hero — things look good but aren't (false gain); new crisis.
- **F:** Ends Act 2 on **false victory or "all is lost"** — the worse, the better for emotional stories.
- **G:** Shortest; urgency and motion; the climax. In drama the hero completes a **u-turn**: ending wanting the opposite of what they wanted at the start.
- **H:** Wrap only if the climax wasn't decisive.

## Hero's journey (Vogler, compressed)
Ordinary World → Call → Refusal → Mentor → Threshold → Tests/Allies/Enemies → Approach → **Ordeal** (death-and-rebirth) → Reward → Road Back → **Resurrection** (final test, theme proven) → Return with the Elixir. Departure → initiation → return; transformation is the plot.

## Wilder's structural rules (via NYFA)
- **"Grab 'em by the throat and never let 'em go"** — open with the killer scene on page one; drip-feed exposition later.
- **"Develop a clean line of action"** — visible objective + plan for the protagonist (extend to every character). No motivation → no conflict → no audience care.
- **"Know where you're going"** — map the plot before writing (note cards, beats); characters who forget their purpose lose the audience.
- **"If you have a problem with the third act, the real problem is in the first act"** — weak endings are weak setups. Fix beginnings, not endings.
- **"The third act must build, build, build in tempo and action until the last event, and then — that's it. Don't hang around."** One ending.

## Climax mechanics
- The hero must act using what the journey taught them — the ending is earned.
- Every payoff was planted (objects, lines, habits).
- The theme is proven in action, not stated in dialogue.
